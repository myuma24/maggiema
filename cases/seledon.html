UI/UX CASE STUDY

Susie Seledon Literacy Fund

OVERVIEW

The Susie Seledon Literacy Fund is a website built from the ground up for a children's literacy program providing free books to families in Gilroy, CA. Created in memory of Susie Seledon by her husband Ed, the fund partners with the Gilroy Foundation, the Santa Clara County Library District, and Dolly Parton's Imagination Library to deliver age-appropriate books to children ages 0–5 every month at no cost to families. The site needed to communicate the fund's mission clearly while honoring the personal story behind it.

MY ROLE

I led the end-to-end UI/UX design, from brand identity and mood boarding through high-fidelity design and developer handoff. A core part of this project was managing design direction across four stakeholder groups; the Gilroy Foundation (client), the donor, and two partner library organizations.

APPROACH

Understanding the Mission and Audience

Before any visual design began, I worked to understand the fund's mission, goals, and audience — primarily parents and guardians of young children in Gilroy. The biggest early challenge wasn't visual, it was structural: this project had four distinct stakeholder groups, each with a different relationship to the fund. The Gilroy Foundation served as the primary client and main point of communication, Ed (the donor) had a personal vision for honoring his wife's memory, and both the Santa Clara County Library District and Dolly Parton's Imagination Library needed the site to accurately represent their roles as program partners.

INSIGHTS

With four stakeholders providing weekly design input, the priority shifted from just designing pages to building a flexible process that could absorb new requests without losing the project's core direction. How might we create a brand and site that honored Ed's personal vision while clearly serving the practical needs of three partner organizations?

Mood Board & Inspiration

Establishing a Visual Direction

Since the fund had no existing visual identity, I started by gathering inspiration around warmth, childhood literacy, and approachable family-facing design — researching how other children's education and literacy brands balance playfulness with credibility.

Logo Exploration

Designing the Mark

Three logo directions were explored — a circular icon with a horizontal wordmark, a stacked lockup, and a silhouette paired with the wordmark — each tested across color and reversed treatments to ensure flexibility across the site and print materials. The final identity centers on a simple illustrated silhouette of a child reading, paired with "Seledon Blue" and "Seledon Yellow" as the core brand colors, Life Savers as the display typeface, and Instrument Sans as the supporting typeface for body content.

Develop

Initial Concepts

With the brand system in place, I designed three distinct homepage directions to present to stakeholders, each interpreting the brand differently — from a softer teal-led layout, to a bold yellow hero treatment, to a warmer gradient approach pairing teal and green. Presenting multiple directions side by side gave all four stakeholder groups a shared reference point to react to, which helped surface alignment (and disagreement) early, before high-fidelity work began.

High Fidelity

Designing Through a Single Channel

Once the bold yellow hero direction was selected, we scoped the first build to two pages: a homepage and an "About" page telling the story of Susie Seledon herself — a teacher who spent her career making an impact on the children she taught, and the inspiration behind the fund Ed created in her memory. Feedback didn't come to us directly. Our project manager presented our designs to the Gilroy Foundation, who relayed them to Ed, and his responses came back through the same chain in reverse. Every round of feedback had to travel through three people before it reached us, which meant ambiguous notes were common and small misunderstandings could take a full review cycle to surface and correct.

The Copy and Data Problem

The Foundation hadn't provided any final copy or data when design began, so early sections were placeholder text built around their one clear directive: "emphasize privacy." Without source copy to react to, several rounds of revisions amounted to guessing at tone until the Foundation finally sent final verbiage to drop in directly.

The testimonials section had a similar miscommunication: we initially designed it to showcase feedback about the program itself, but the Foundation's intent was for it to feature testimonials from children Susie had personally taught — a section meant to honor her story, not the fund's outcomes. Once clarified, I redesigned the section to read more like a tribute, prioritizing personal quotes and warmth over the more clinical "program review" framing it originally had.

Visualizing Enrollment Data Accurately

The most technically interesting challenge was the enrollment data section. Ed initially wanted a timeline comparing Gilroy enrollment against county and national numbers. When he later asked to compare Gilroy specifically against the county's individual cities, I redesigned it as a pie chart. But this surfaced a real data-integrity issue: Gilroy's enrollment numbers update quarterly, while county-wide data only updates twice a year. Displaying both on the same chart side by side risked misleading viewers into thinking the numbers were equally current. I flagged this and we removed the mixed comparison, keeping the pie chart limited to same-cadence city data. Ed then requested the original bar chart comparison back — Gilroy vs. county vs. country — so the final design needed to visually distinguish data freshness rather than removing the comparison altogether.

Scope Expansion Under a Hard Deadline

Midway through revisions, the Santa Clara County Library District and Dolly Parton's Imagination Library both submitted their own requests via a shared slide deck: additional copy changes, a new page, an optional literacy resources page, and their logos added across nearly every section. This arrived alongside a hard launch deadline — the boy featured as the face of the brand, Mattie, was moving away soon, and the Foundation wanted the site live before he left. That gave us three working days to implement every outstanding request and launch by the following Monday.

Final Designs

Takeaways

Key Learnings

  • Indirect Feedback Loops Require Extra Clarity: With feedback passing through three people before reaching me, I learned to ask more pointed, specific questions in every handoff rather than open-ended ones, since ambiguity compounds the more hands a message passes through.
  • Data Integrity Is a Design Decision: Catching the mismatched update cadence between Gilroy and county-wide enrollment data was a reminder that visual clarity isn't enough — a chart can be well-designed and still mislead if the underlying data isn't comparable.
  • Designing for Scope Creep: Two additional stakeholder organizations joined mid-project with their own asks delivered via slide deck. Building flexible, modular page components from the start made it possible to absorb new logo placements and content sections without redesigning from scratch under a three-day deadline.

Next Steps

  • Continuing to support the fund with ongoing design requests as enrollment data and partner needs evolve.
  • Exploring a clearer visual treatment to communicate data freshness directly on the chart (e.g. labeling update cadence) so future data requests don't reopen the same accuracy question.
