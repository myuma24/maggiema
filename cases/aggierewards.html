UI/UX CASE STUDY

AggieRewards

OVERVIEW

AggieRewards was the product of a three week long design sprint hosted by Design Interactive at UC Davis. An app that incentivizes students to dine on campus. With our design thinking, we won an award for Most Human Centered Design.

MY ROLE

I collaborate with three other designers for a 3-week design sprint. I served as one of the main designers translating research into a high-fidelity prototype and worked with the team to establish consistency.

AWARDS

Most Human Centered Design

Objective: How might we design a mobile app that enhances the dining experience for UC Davis students by incentivizing and rewarding visiting on-campus food destinations?

RESEARCH

Interviews

We began by conducting a few interviews to guage what UC Davis students and their preferences in rewards systems were. We noted concerns regarding the lack of variety in campus food options. Additionally, the UC Davis "Aggie Cash" system was perceived as exclusive to freshmen students who purchase meal plans, limiting its accessibility and appeal to other student demographics.

This helped me gain insight into how we could create an effective loyalty rewards program for students.

Survey Insights

We created a survey to gauge the frequency of students' participation in on-campus dining, their likelihood of engaging in potential loyalty programs, the challenges they face using these programs, and more.

Competitive Audit

A competitive audit was conducted to analyze loyalty programs and evaluate their pros and cons. We chose reward programs based on what survey participants answered which was Teaspoon, McDonald's, and Sephora.

CONCEPT

Core Features We Proposed

We designed AggieRewards around existing UC Davis dining habits and operations, focusing on incentives and pickup ordering rather than changing the current dining workflow.

  • Stamp-card rewards: Users would earn a stamp after visiting three selected campus dining locations, with a reward such as $10 off any purchase at MU restaurants and markets.
  • Points system: Users would also earn points from purchases at any on-campus dining location as a long-term rewards system.
  • Quarterly reset: The stamp card would reset every academic quarter with new locations and spots to visit.
  • Pickup ordering only: Users could place scheduled or standard pickup orders. We chose not to include delivery because most campus dining locations are student-operated, and delivery would change the existing UC Davis dining experience.

USER TESTING

User Testing Notes

After presenting our mid-fidelity screens and having our testers complete two tasks: access rewards and order a drink, we gathered that while the concept was well received, the overall flow needed stronger coherency and clearer reward logic.

1. OVERALL COHERENCY

Testers felt the experience lacked visual and functional cohesiveness across screens. Some features appeared disconnected, which made it harder to understand the primary value of the app at a glance.

2. REWARDS SYSTEM CONFUSION

Users were unclear whether the points system and stamp card led to the same rewards or represented two separate reward systems. Our intention was for them to be different: the stamp card would reset each quarter, while points would never expire.

3. TOO MANY REWARD PATHS

We discovered that having both points and a stamp card overcomplicated the experience. Maintaining two reward mechanics added cognitive load and made the core incentive harder to understand quickly.

Takeaways

Through discussion, we identified a major issue with the stamp card model: if a user disliked one of the assigned dining locations, they would have to wait until the end of the quarter for the card to reset. Even then, they might receive another location they still do not prefer. This conflicted with earlier research findings showing that students disliked being limited to certain places. A stamp card works best when tied to locations users already visit, but randomized locations make completion less likely.

Key Takeaway: A rewards experience should feel simple and immediately understandable. Our testing showed that clarity in the reward structure mattered more than offering multiple incentive systems at once.

HIGH-FIDELITY

Iterations

Using feedback from our mid-fidelity review, we refined the high-fidelity interface to improve clarity, consistency, and usability.

Key revisions included:

  • New color palette: The usage of our colors did not pass WCAG, we updated our palette to reflect this.
  • Simplified rewards system: Based on user testing, we removed the stamp card and focused on points. To keep exploration rewarding, we introduced bonus points (e.g., 1.5× points) at select locations and for targeted dining spots.
  • Reworked home page: We redesigned the home page to align with the streamlined rewards experience.
  • Checkout clarity: One particular tester noted that the checkout screen lacked distinction ex. the scheduling buttons looked too similiar.
  • Updated overall UI: We refreshed the UI across the app to match the new system and visual direction, including checkout and completion screens.

New Color Palette

We replaced the original pastel blue with a warmer yellow to better complement UC Davis’s blue-and-gold visual identity. This change improved contrast and created a more cohesive look across the interface.

NEW

Simplified Rewards System & Home Page Rework

To reduce confusion, we consolidated the rewards experience into a single points-based system. Instead of splitting progress between points and a stamp card, all purchases now contribute toward the same system, making it easier to understand and helping users earn rewards faster.

We removed the stamp card and map from the home page and replaced them with recommended locations that refresh periodically. This keeps exploration rewarding by offering small bonus points for visiting (or revisiting) featured spots—without making students feel pressured to try something they don’t want.

Takeaways

Next Steps

If this project continued beyond the sprint, the next step would be to further validate the revised rewards experience and expand the product into a more complete end-to-end dining flow.

Next steps would include:

  • Conducting another round of user testing on the updated points-based rewards system to confirm that it feels clearer and more intuitive
  • Refining the recommended locations feature to better balance discovery, personalization, and bonus-point incentives
  • Expanding the ordering flow with more detailed states for customization, checkout, scheduling, and order confirmation
  • Exploring edge cases such as unavailable menu items, incomplete purchases, and reward redemption limitations

I would also want to further explore how rewards could feel more personalized over time. Since student dining habits vary widely, future iterations could better adapt featured locations to match user preferences without making the experience feel restrictive.

Future opportunities could include:

  • Personalized recommendations based on dining habits and preferred locations
  • Smarter bonus-point campaigns tied to under-visited campus spots
  • Clearer reward redemption moments within the ordering flow
